## 4. Key Risks Over the Next Five Years

### Regulatory and Antitrust Overhang (Primarily Alphabet)

Alphabet faces a more acute regulatory risk profile. Following the DOJ's search antitrust case, 

the court ruled that Google will be subject to several behavioral remedies, including a prohibition of exclusive contracts relating to the distribution of Google Search, the Chrome browser, and certain AI products,

 though it 

rejected the plaintiffs' proposals for an immediate divestiture of Chrome and a contingent divestiture of Android.

 Both sides have since appealed: 

the Justice Department and 35 states will appeal the September 2025 court ruling that allowed Google to keep its Chrome browser,

 while 

Google itself filed an 111-page appeal arguing the lower court made "as basic an error of antitrust law as a court can make," and asking the appeals court to overturn the remedies.

 This litigation will likely run for years and creates a persistent overhang, including the risk — however diminished for now — of more severe structural remedies down the line.

### AI Infrastructure Spending and the "Bubble" Debate (Both Companies)

The most significant shared risk is whether the enormous capex now being deployed will generate adequate returns. Industry-wide, 

the five largest US cloud and AI infrastructure providers have collectively committed to spending between $660 billion and $690 billion on capital expenditure in 2026, nearly doubling 2025 levels.

 Critics point to a widening gap between spending and depreciation: 

the five biggest hyperscalers spent about $412 billion on capex in 2025, with 2026 estimates running to roughly $760 billion, yet the AI capex depreciation and amortization expected to be recognized against all that spending in 2026 is only about $211 billion.

 This is projected to severely compress near-term free cash flow — 

combined free cash flow at the five companies is projected to fall 91% to about $16 billion in 2026, while net income is projected to rise 25% to roughly $506 billion.

 There are also concerns about asset life assumptions: 

Michael Burry has argued that accelerator fleets have an economic life closer to two to three years than the five-to-six-year schedules in use, which could mean industry depreciation is understated by roughly $176 billion over 2026-2028

 — though this specific estimate remains disputed. For context, Microsoft's own capex has already shown strain on cash generation: 

capex went from $44.48 billion in fiscal 2024 to $115.95 billion in fiscal 2026, with its share of operating cash flow climbing from 38% to 63%, while free cash flow slipped.



That said, demand signals for both companies remain robust rather than showing cracks — Microsoft's 

remaining performance obligations spiked 51% to $392 billion

 and Alphabet's cloud backlog has repeatedly surged, which argues that current spending is being met by real contracted demand, not merely speculative buildout. The risk is more about the pace/timing mismatch between spending and revenue recognition than a lack of end demand.

## 6. Synthesis: Five-Year Outlook

**Microsoft** enters the next five years as the more diversified, cash-generative enterprise technology franchise with best-in-class distribution (Windows, Microsoft 365, LinkedIn, Dynamics) and an outsized, high-conviction bet on OpenAI that has already turned a ~$13 billion investment into a stake worth well over $200 billion on paper. Its growth is extremely visible and contractually underpinned (the $250 billion OpenAI Azure commitment, $392 billion in RPO), but it is also increasingly a capital-intensive infrastructure story, with capex approaching $190–200 billion annually and free cash flow under genuine pressure.

**Alphabet** enters the period with arguably the strongest recent growth inflection among the hyperscalers — Google Cloud's acceleration to 82% growth and a $514 billion backlog are remarkable for a business of its scale — combined with unique structural assets (TPUs, DeepMind, YouTube, Waymo) that Microsoft cannot easily replicate. Its core risk is less about demand and more about regulatory overhang from ongoing antitrust appeals, and the sheer scale of capex it must now fund externally via equity issuance.

Both companies are making history-sized bets on the same AI infrastructure supercycle, and their near-term stock performance is now explicitly tied to whether that multi-hundred-billion-dollar spending converts into durable, high-margin revenue within the backlog-to-revenue windows management teams have described (roughly 24 months for Google Cloud's backlog). Given their scale, balance sheet strength, and the breadth of monetizable AI surface area each controls (productivity software and cloud for Microsoft; search, cloud, video, and autonomous vehicles for Alphabet), both appear structurally well positioned over a five-year horizon — but Alphabet's combination of accelerating cloud growth, silicon independence, and optionality in Waymo gives it a somewhat broader set of call options on how the AI era unfolds, while Microsoft offers more contracted, visible near-term cash flows anchored to enterprise software stickiness and its OpenAI economics.
